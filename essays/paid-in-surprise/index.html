You’re paid in surprise, not pleasure

14 November 2026

In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys as they received juice. At first the neurons fired when the juice arrived. Once a light reliably predicted the juice, they fired at the light and stayed silent for the juice itself. When the light came and no juice followed, firing dropped below baseline.

That is not a pleasure signal. It is an error signal: what you got minus what you expected. It matches, almost term for term, the temporal-difference error that computer scientists had devised separately to train learning machines.

Wanting is not liking. Rats stripped of dopamine still show pleasure when sugar touches their tongue, but they won’t cross the cage to get it and will starve beside food. Dopamine builds the pull, not the pleasure. Because the two run on different circuitry, they can come apart, leaving you craving something intensely while barely enjoying it. That gap is the signature of addiction, and a fair description of stress eating, where the craving is loud and the food barely registers.

Addiction hacks the error term. Natural rewards get predicted away: the tenth bite teaches nothing, so the signal fades. In one influential model, drugs like cocaine raise dopamine chemically, whatever you expected. The brain reads “better than predicted” every time, so the value of the drug, and of every cue pointing to it, keeps climbing. Gambling runs a milder version with no chemical at all, because a reward you can’t predict is one you never finish learning about.

Habits outlive their reasons. With repetition, control shifts from circuits that ask “is this worth it?” to circuits that simply run cue, then action. This is why your hand is on the phone the moment it buzzes, before you have decided anything, and why redesigning the cue beats fighting the urge.

The same logic applies to wellbeing. When researchers tracked mood moment to moment during a gambling task, happiness followed not how much people had won but whether they had just done better than expected. No level of anything stays good. The system normalises whatever you have and reports only the change, because it was built to keep you moving, not to make you content.

So the lever isn’t more reward. Pick pursuits where progress keeps surprising you, such as skills, craft, and people. Leave some expectations low enough to be beaten. And learn to tell the pull from the pleasure: the itch is not evidence that scratching will feel good.
